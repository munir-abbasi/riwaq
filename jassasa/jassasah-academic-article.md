## Abstract

The *ḥadīth of al-Jassāsah* recounts an island encounter with an enigmatic informant and a chained figure identified as the Dajjāl, embedded in reports of a prophetic sermon narrated by Fāṭimah bint Qays. This study was carried out as an empirical proof-of-concept to evaluate the combined functionality of **Masar** as an automated takhrīj and pericope-alignment engine and **Riwaq** as an Isnād-cum-Matn analysis (ICMA) and stemma engine. Analyzing a primary corpus of twenty records yielding twenty-two distinct compiler paths across nine classical collections, the pipeline establishes ʿĀmir al-Shaʿbī (d. ca. 103–105 AH / 721–723 CE) as the primary structural common link ($\mathrm{Conf} = 0.6743$, bundle coverage $86.4\%$, bypass ratio $4.5\%$), with collateral branches through Abū Salamah (Medina) and Yaḥyā ibn Yaʿmar (Khurasan/Basra) demonstrating early multi-regional transmission beyond Kufa. Textual collation across seven narrative episodes reveals three traveler profiles—Tamīm sailing personally with Lakhmid/Judhamite companions, Tamīm's kinsmen sailing without him, and unnamed regional voyagers—alongside three distinct lexical registers for al-Jassāsah: a zoomorphic beast (*dābbah* / *al-Jassās*), an ambiguous hair-covered human (*insān* / *shayʾ*), and an explicitly female personification (*imraʾah* / *jāriyah*), the latter concentrated in collateral lines. Crucially, automated pericope alignment demonstrates that the kinsmen reading (*banū ʿamm*) is attested across four distinct pupil branches radiating from al-Shaʿbī, refuting the classical suspicion that this formulation was an idiosyncratic corruption exclusive to Mujālid ibn Saʿīd. Within the ICMA framework, this establishes a conditional transmission horizon in late first-century Kufa, distinct from third-century literary compilation dates and later physical manuscript survival, demonstrating the viability of a coupled takhrīj-ICMA computational architecture for scalable hadith studies.

## Introduction

In its most familiar form, the Jassāsah narrative is embedded within Fāṭimah bint Qays's recollection of a public sermon. The Prophet relates news brought by Tamīm al-Dārī, whose maritime story features a strange hairy being, a chained man, questions about places in Palestine and its environs, and the latter's identification as the Dajjāl. The long version in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* connects the account with questions about Mecca and Medina and a gesture pointing east [1]. Yet the surviving reports differ in their framing, participants, scene descriptions, sequence of interrogations, and endings. Reading one long account as the fixed original would conceal those differences.

The historical question is consequently twofold: what transmission structures do the extant witnesses actually report, and which textual features correlate with those structures? A common-link analysis can describe branching among named transmitters; an isnād-cum-matn analysis must additionally test whether particular readings travel with those branches. Neither operation by itself establishes the reported event as an independently observed occurrence or determines the date at which a detail first circulated.

## Corpus and method

This investigation synthesizes a primary source-text corpus and source audit comprising nineteen separately indexed digital attestations: Muslim 2942a–d; Abū Dāwūd 4325–4328; al-Tirmidhī 2253; Ibn Mājah 4074; al-Nasāʾī's *al-Sunan al-Kubrā* 4453 in the consulted display; Aḥmad 26066 in the Wikisource numbering; Ibn Abī Shaybah 40425; Ibn Ḥibbān 6787; al-Ṭayālisī 1751; and al-Ṭabarānī's *al-Muʿjam al-Kabīr* 956, 960, 967 and 968 [1–12]. The primary source audit preserves source-form Arabic, normalized isnāds, and verified digital locators across all nineteen attestations. Collection numbering follows the displayed digital editions and may not coincide with other editions.

A *witness* or *attestation* here denotes one separately displayed entry in a collection, while a *compiler path* denotes a distinguishable isnād leading to that compiler. In the nineteen-attestation baseline, Muslim 2942a and 2942c each cite two distinct teachers within a single textual entry, yielding twenty parsable compiler paths across eighteen encoded records. Al-Ṭabarānī 967 constitutes the nineteenth textual witness; its compound isnād conjunction (“عن قتادة، عن الشعبي، وإبراهيم بن عامر، عن الشعبي”) is retained for textual collation but left unparsed in the baseline graph to avoid artificial path inflation. The twenty parsed paths resolve into twelve lower-branch classes: nine pupil lines radiating from al-Shaʿbī, two collateral approaches to Fāṭimah, and a distinct Jābir comparator. Crucially, these lower branches represent downstream transmission paths from their respective authorities rather than twelve independent eyewitnesses to the Prophet.

Comparison was organized across seven canonical episodes ($P_1 \dots P_7$): the occasion of the sermon ($P_1$); identification of the voyagers ($P_2$); island landing and encounter with al-Jassāsah ($P_3$); description and binding of the captive ($P_4$); geographical and religious inquiries regarding Baysān, Lake Ṭabariyyah, Zughar, the Arabian Prophet, and in some recensions, Persia ($P_5$); messianic self-identification and sanctuary boundaries ($P_6$); and the Prophet's ratification, orientation gesture, and closing remarks ($P_7$). A source's cross-reference to a previously quoted longer report (e.g., *wa-dhakara al-ḥadīth*) is treated as an editorial abridgment rather than evidence that omitted episodes were unknown to its transmitter.

Source verification was conducted through digitized Arabic text editions of classical standard reference printings (specifically digitized recensions hosted on Sunnah.com, Islamweb Library, Shamela, and Wikisource, reflecting standard scholarly editions such as Fuʾād ʿAbd al-Bāqī's *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim*, Muḥyī al-Dīn ʿAbd al-Ḥamīd's *Sunan Abī Dāwūd*, Aḥmad Shākir's *Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī*, Saʿd al-Shathrī's *Muṣannaf Ibn Abī Shaybah*, Shuʿayb al-Arnāʾūṭ's *Ṣaḥīḥ Ibn Ḥibbān*, and Ḥamdī ʿAbd al-Majīd al-Salafī's *al-Muʿjam al-Kabīr*). Physical paper volumes, photographic page facsimiles, and unedited manuscript folios were not directly examined in this study. A supplementary direct source verification audit incorporated two further entries from al-Ṭabarānī's *al-Muʿjam al-Kabīr*: (i) al-Ṭabarānī 964 (the Dāwūd–Ḥammād route preserving an inquest on Persia), contributing one compiler path; and (ii) al-Ṭabarānī 967, which resolves the conjoined isnād (“عن قتادة، عن الشعبي، وإبراهيم بن عامر، عن الشعبي”) by encoding the primary, attested Qatādah branch as one verified compiler path (`jass-tabarani-967-route`). Rather than artificially inflating the path denominator by encoding the unverified secondary conjunct (Ibrāhīm ibn ʿĀmir) as an independent graph path, 967 is resolved to a single compiler path. Consequently, the expanded corpus consists of twenty records ($18 + 2$) yielding exactly **twenty-two compiler paths** ($20 + 1 + 1 = 22$) [14, 15].

### Computational Reproducibility and Metric Formulations

To ensure methodological transparency and reproducibility, the computational pipeline integrates the Masar Takhrīj Engine with Riwaq's analytical framework under standardized schemas (`schema_version: 1`) [13, 15]:

1. **Orthographic Normalization:** Arabic source strings undergo deterministic normalization: short vowels (*tashkīl*) and tatwīl are stripped, hamzated and bare alifs are unified (`[إأآا] → ا`), terminal forms are harmonized (`[يى] → ي`, `[ةه] → ه`), and non-alphabetic punctuation is removed.
2. **Pericope Segmentation & Token-Jaccard Scoring:** Canonical episode spans ($P_1 \dots P_7$) are delimited by zero-based character offsets. Local alignment between a witness span $S_w$ and canonical episode seed tokens $S_k$ is measured by tokenized Jaccard similarity:
   $$J(S_w, S_k) = \frac{|T(S_w) \cap T(S_k)|}{|T(S_w) \cup T(S_k)|}$$
   where $T(S)$ represents the unique token set of span $S$. Compiler abridgment notices are classified as structural references rather than textual omissions, preventing artificial penalties against editorial summaries.
3. **Common Link Confidence Formulation:** In Riwaq's implementation (`clpcl-analyzer.js`), candidate common-link confidence $\operatorname{Conf}(v)$ is calculated via a multi-signal bounded linear function:
   $$\operatorname{Conf}(v) = \operatorname{clamp}\Big(0.30 \cdot S_1(v) + 0.25 \cdot S_2(v) + 0.15 \cdot S_3(v) + 0.20 \cdot S_4(v) + 0.10 \cdot S_5(v) - 0.20 \cdot P_1(v) - 0.10 \cdot P_2(v) - 0.05 \cdot P_3(v), 0, 1\Big)$$
   where $\operatorname{norm}(x, \text{lo}, \text{hi}) = \operatorname{clamp}\left(\frac{x - \text{lo}}{\text{hi} - \text{lo}}, 0, 1\right)$ and $\operatorname{clamp}(x, 0, 1) = \min(\max(x, 0), 1)$.
   The constitutive signals ($S_1 \dots S_5$) and negative penalties ($P_1 \dots P_3$) represent:
   - $S_1(v) = \operatorname{norm}(\text{fan\_out}(v), 3, 8)$: normalized count of immediate pupil lines radiating from node $v$;
   - $S_2(v) = \text{bundle\_coverage}(v)$: proportion of total bundle compiler paths traversing node $v$;
   - $S_3(v) = \operatorname{norm}(\text{collector\_diversity}(v), 2, 8)$: normalized count of distinct canonical compilers receiving reports through $v$;
   - $S_4(v) = \text{pre\_single\_strand\_ratio}(v)$: proportion of pre-CL authorities exhibiting single-strand transmission ($0.0$ for al-Shaʿbī);
   - $S_5(v) = \text{matn\_coherence}(v)$: mean pairwise token-Jaccard similarity across aligned pericopes among $v$'s pupil branches (falling back to a $0.50$ constant when unsegmented);
   - $P_1(v) = \text{bypass\_ratio}(v)$: proportion of bundle paths reaching earlier authorities without traversing $v$;
   - $P_2(v) = \text{chronology\_conflict\_ratio}(v)$: proportion of transmission links violating biographical death-date sequencing ($0.0$ in this corpus);
   - $P_3(v) = 1 - \text{provenance\_completeness\_ratio}(v)$: penalty for unverified biographical metadata ($0.0$ in this audited corpus).

## The observed transmission network

The dominant configuration is Fāṭimah bint Qays → ʿĀmir al-Shaʿbī → several reported pupils → later transmitters → compilers. The nine al-Shaʿbī pupil labels in the encoded corpus are ʿAbd Allāh ibn Buraydah, Sayyār Abū al-Ḥakam, Ghaylān ibn Jarīr, Abū al-Zinād, Qatādah, Dāwūd ibn Abī Hind, Mujālid ibn Saʿīd, Muḥammad ibn Ayyūb Abū ʿĀṣim, and al-Shaybānī. The last two labels remain subject to the source-level identity qualifications recorded in the audit.

### The Biographical Anchor: ʿĀmir ibn Sharāḥīl al-Shaʿbī (d. ca. 103–105 AH / 721–723 CE)

The structural emergence of ʿĀmir al-Shaʿbī as the principal structural common-link candidate is historically and philologically profound. Al-Shaʿbī was the foremost jurist and traditionalist of Kufa at the turn of the eighth century CE. Born during the caliphate of ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb (ca. 20–31 AH), he lived at the intellectual and political crossroads of the early Islamic empire, serving as an advisor and diplomatic envoy for the Umayyad caliph ʿAbd al-Malik ibn Marwān to the Byzantine court in Constantinople, and subsequently as *qāḍī* of Kufa appointed by ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz [12]. Al-Shaʿbī is reported to have encountered upwards of five hundred Companions of the Prophet, narrating directly from several dozen, including early female authorities such as ʿĀʾishah and Fāṭimah bint Qays. 

Significantly, al-Shaʿbī was renowned for an uncompromising oral memory ethos: classical biographical compendia famously record his declaration, *"I never wrote black on white [ink on parchment], nor did any man narrate a hadith to me that I ever desired him to repeat"* (*mā katabtu sawādan fī bayāḍ...*) [11, 12]. His transmission circle functioned as an inter-regional transit conduit through which Kufan legal and narrative traditions were disseminated across the early Islamic provinces. The nine pupil lines identified in this dataset—spanning Basran ascetics and traditionists like Ghaylān ibn Jarīr, Qatādah, and Dāwūd ibn Abī Hind, to prominent Kufan specialists like Mujālid ibn Saʿīd and Sayyār Abū al-Ḥakam—demonstrate that his teaching assemblies operated as a widespread supra-regional clearinghouse rather than a localized provincial circle.

This fan-out is visible independently of the Riwaq score. The Ibn Buraydah–Ḥusayn al-Muʿallim–ʿAbd al-Wārith line connects Muslim 2942a with Abū Dāwūd 4326 [1, 2]. Sayyār's line through Qurrah ibn Khālid appears in Muslim 2942b, al-Ṭayālisī, and al-Ṭabarānī 968 [1, 9, 10]. Ghaylān and Abū al-Zinād are represented in Muslim's abbreviated notices [1]. The Qatādah branch appears in al-Tirmidhī [3]; the Dāwūd–Ḥammād line in Aḥmad and al-Nasāʾī [5, 6]; and Mujālid's lines in Abū Dāwūd, Ibn Mājah and Ibn Abī Shaybah [2, 4, 7]. These repeated lower chains are observations of transmitted relationships. In isnād-cum-matn analysis, these pupil lines represent downstream partial common links or single strands radiating from al-Shaʿbī; while—under the standard working premise of the method—they indicate that the narrative was circulating in al-Shaʿbī's Kufan milieu, they converge on him and cannot be treated as separate, independent lines reaching back to Fāṭimah or the Prophet.

The broader Fāṭimah network also contains Abū Salamah → al-Zuhrī, attested in Abū Dāwūd 4325, and Yaḥyā ibn Yaʿmar → Ibn Buraydah, attested in Ibn Ḥibbān 6787 [2, 8]. Thus, while al-Shaʿbī is central to the major family sampled, he does not exhaust the reported pathways from Fāṭimah. These two non-Shaʿbī routes constitute genuine collateral lines from Medina and Khurasan/Basra, showing that knowledge of Fāṭimah's tradition was not confined to al-Shaʿbī's Kufan circle. Ibn Buraydah's appearance in both the al-Shaʿbī and Yaḥyā routes also complicates an overly simple picture of separate lines. Abū Dāwūd 4328, through Jābir and Abū Salamah, is retained as a thematic comparator rather than merged into Fāṭimah's formal isnād [2].

Within these branches further subdivisions can be observed. Qurrah leads to three sampled pupils; Mujālid to Ismāʿīl ibn Abī Khālid and Abū Usāmah; and the latter Ismāʿīl line to different later transmitters. This structure helps formulate hypotheses about where textual clusters may have been transmitted. It describes transmission branching, not eyewitness verification.

## Variation in the narrative frame and the voyagers

The sermon occasion is unstable across the displayed readings. Muslim's expansive version situates the episode within Fāṭimah's personal account and a smiling public address; other notices place the summons after a delayed evening prayer or an unusual ẓuhr ascent to the pulpit. Ibn Mājah emphasizes the Prophet's pleasure at the news and a missed midday rest [1, 2, 4]. Such differences might reflect abbreviated narration, alternative framing or repeated retelling. The corpus cannot settle which explanation is correct.

The identity of those who made the voyage is even more consequential, exhibiting three distinct narrative profiles:
1. **Tamīm sailing personally with companions:** Muslim 2942a and Abū Dāwūd 4326 (via Ibn Buraydah) explicitly record that Tamīm narrated that he embarked in a sea vessel with thirty men of Lakhm and Judhām (*rakiba fī safīnah baḥriyyah maʿa thalāthīna rajulan min Lakhm wa-Judhām*). The Dāwūd ibn Abī Hind branch (al-Nasāʾī 4453, Aḥmad 26066, al-Ṭabarānī 964) similarly records Tamīm sailing with his companions (*aṣḥāb*). In an abbreviated formulation, Ghaylān ibn Jarīr (Muslim 2942c) likewise preserves Tamīm's personal landing on the island seeking water (*fasaqaṭa ilā jazīrah fa-kharaja ilayhā yaltamisu al-māʾ*).
2. **Tamīm's kinsmen sailing without him:** Muslim 2942b (via Sayyār) states that kinsmen of Tamīm took to the sea (*inna banī ʿamm li-Tamīm al-Dārī rakibū fī al-baḥr*), echoed in al-Ṭayālisī 1751 and al-Ṭabarānī 968. Muḥammad ibn Ayyūb Abū ʿĀṣim (al-Ṭabarānī 956) and al-Shaybānī (al-Ṭabarānī 960) likewise preserve the kinsmen framing (*banū ʿamm lah* / *nāsan min banī ʿamm Tamīm*). Within the Mujālid branch, Ibn Mājah 4074 reads that a cousin of Tamīm informed him (*ibn ʿamm li-Tamīm akhbaranī*), while Ibn Abī Shaybah 40425 records a party of his kinsmen (*rahṭan min banī ʿammih*).
3. **Unnamed regional voyagers:** Al-Tirmidhī 2253 (via Qatādah) describes people from the people of Palestine (*nāsan min ahl Filasṭīn*) boarding a ship, without identifying Tamīm as one of the passengers; and Abū Dāwūd 4325 (via Abū Salamah) presents Tamīm recounting news from an unnamed man on an island (*ʿan rajul kāna fī jazīrah*).

In classical *jarḥ wa-taʿdīl* literature, Mujālid ibn Saʿīd was frequently characterized as weak (*ḍaʿīf*) and prone to confused transmission (*taghayyur* / *awhām*) [11], prompting commentators and early takhrīj analyses to suspect that attributing the voyage to a cousin or kinsmen was an idiosyncratic defect introduced by Mujālid. However, the multi-branch evidence demonstrates that kinsmen language (*banū ʿamm*) is attested across four distinct pupil lines of al-Shaʿbī: Sayyār, Muḥammad ibn Ayyūb, al-Shaybānī, and Mujālid. Consequently, kinsmen attribution alone is **not** a diagnostic marker of Mujālid. A narrower grouping of the Mujālid readings can be made from the *combination* of a kinsmen account with the distressed captive, a question about the Arabs' religious condition, and a particular sequence of the geographical questions [4, 7]. Even that grouping remains a textual hypothesis pending a larger collation.

## The Jassāsah and the captive

Collation of the encountered entity across the twenty-witness corpus reveals three distinct lexical registers, rather than a uniform binary:

1. **The Zoomorphic Beast (*dābbah* / *al-Jassās*):** In Muslim 2942a (Ibn Buraydah), the figure is “دابة أهلب كثير الشعر”, a heavily hairy creature whose front and back cannot readily be distinguished [1]. This zoomorphic designation (*dābbah*) is preserved in Muslim 2942b (Sayyār), al-Ṭayālisī 1751, al-Ṭabarānī 968, al-Tirmidhī 2253 (Qatādah), and the Dāwūd ibn Abī Hind branch (al-Nasāʾī 4453, Aḥmad 26066, al-Ṭabarānī 964). Al-Ṭabarānī 967 adds the unique masculine nominal form *al-Jassās* [1, 3, 5, 6, 9, 10, 14]. Importantly, *al-Jassās* in al-Ṭabarānī 967 functions as a masculine nominal designation rather than an explicit zoological description; grammatical form (masculine agent noun) and inferred creature morphology must therefore be treated as distinct analytical variables in textual collation.
2. **The Ambiguous Hairy Human or Shape (*insān* / *shayʾ* / *shabaḥ*):** A significant cluster within al-Shaʿbī's Kufan transmission circle describes the entity not as an animal, but as a hair-shrouded human or indefinite entity whose gender is visually obscured. Crucially, Muslim 2942c (Ghaylān ibn Jarīr $\to$ al-Shaʿbī) explicitly records that Tamīm landed on an island seeking water and *"met a human dragging his hair"* («فَلَقِيَ إِنْسَانًا يَجُرُّ شَعَرَهُ») [1]. Similarly, al-Ṭabarānī 960 (al-Shaybānī $\to$ al-Shaʿbī) records: *"they came upon a human, they did not know whether male or female from the profusion of hair"* («فَإِذَا هُمْ بِإِنْسَانٍ لَا يَدْرُونَ أَذَكَرٌ هُوَ أَمْ أُنْثَى مِنْ كَثْرَةِ الشَّعْرِ») [10]. Al-Ṭabarānī 956 (Abū ʿĀṣim $\to$ al-Shaʿbī) likewise reads *"they met an entity, they did not know whether a man or a woman"* («فَلَقُوا شَيْئًا لَا يَدْرُونَ رَجُلًا هُوَ أَوْ امْرَأَةً») [10]. This ambiguous human register is also shared by the Mujālid branch: Ibn Mājah 4074 reads *"a shaggy human form dragging his hair"* («شَبَحًا يَجُرُّ شَعَرَهُ»), while Ibn Abī Shaybah 40425 has *"a black, shaggy thing"* («شَيْئًا أَسْوَدَ أَهْلَبَ») [4, 7].
3. **The Explicit Female Human (*imraʾah* / *jāriyah*):** Outside al-Shaʿbī's circle, the independent collateral lines explicitly introduce female human personifications: Abū Dāwūd 4325 (via Abū Salamah in Medina) reads “بامرأة تجر شعرها”, a woman dragging her hair [2], while Ibn Ḥibbān 6787 (via Yaḥyā ibn Yaʿmar in Khurasan/Basra) reads “جارية تجر شعرها”, a young woman or maiden dragging her hair [8]. In Abū Dāwūd 4328 (the Jābir comparator), the female characterization is supplied in a reported transmitter's explanatory dialogue rather than the primary encounter narrative [2].

These textual alignments disprove any claim of rigid zoomorphic uniformity in al-Shaʿbī's Kufan circle: al-Shaʿbī's pupils oscillate between the zoomorphic beast (*dābbah*) and the hair-covered human or entity (*insān* / *shayʾ*). Within the present sampled corpus, the *explicitly female personification* (*imraʾah* / *jāriyah*) is uniquely concentrated in the non-Shaʿbī collateral lines of Medina and Khurasan; whether this concentration reflects an early regional differentiation or an artifact of surviving textual selection remains an open question for broader manuscript collation.

Abū Dāwūd 4328 is revealing about attribution levels. A female characterization of al-Jassāsah is supplied in a reported transmitter's explanatory dialogue, rather than appearing as the same sentence of the island encounter as in 4325 [2]. That explanatory gloss is evidence about reception and interpretation, but should not automatically be inserted into the original speech of a narrator in every other version.

The captive likewise ranges from an unusually large, tightly bound man to an elderly, distressed figure or a man whose motion or leap inspires fear. Variation is found both in descriptive vocabulary and in sequence: some readings identify him as the Dajjāl immediately; others place substantial questioning before the identification [1–8]. No single term should be harmonized with another without a separate lexical and manuscript inquiry.

## Questions, order, and additions

The fullest Muslim account arranges the inquiries about the date palms of Baysān, Lake Ṭabariyyah, the spring of Zughar, and the Arabian Prophet in that order [1]. But the order is not invariant. The compact al-Tirmidhī recension asks first about Zughar and then a lake and Baysān [3]. Ibn Mājah and Ibn Abī Shaybah place questions about the Prophet and Arabs before Zughar, Baysān and Ṭabariyyah [4, 7]. The Dāwūd branch in Aḥmad and al-Nasāʾī places the prophetic question and a question about Persia before the geographic sequence [5, 6]. The consulted al-Ṭabarānī witnesses add further permutations [10].

These observations show both an identifiable shared story and considerable freedom in how its interrogation scene is organized. The question about Persia is demonstrably branch-concentrated in the Dāwūd–Ḥammād reports (*al-Sunan al-Kubrā* 4453, *Musnad Aḥmad* 26066, *al-Muʿjam al-Kabīr* 964). However, the precise historical moment and mechanism of its emergence remain unknown: it could reflect an interpolation or regional gloss during mid-second-century Basran transmission (e.g., around Ḥammād ibn Salamah, d. 167 AH), or alternatively an authentic early variant not preserved in other branches; the surviving digital evidence establishes its branch concentration but cannot fix its exact date of origin.

Similarly, when the Mujālid cluster (*Sunan Ibn Mājah* 4074, *Musannaf Ibn Abī Shaybah* 40425, *Sunan Abī Dāwūd* 4327) is compared systematically against all other sampled branches, its distinctive profile emerges not from the kinsmen reading alone—which is shared with three other al-Shaʿbī lines (Sayyār, Abū ʿĀṣim, al-Shaybānī)—but from a unique *constellation of co-occurring narrative features*: (1) the kinsmen framing ($P_2$), (2) the ambiguous human or shaggy form ($P_3$, shared with Ghaylān, Abū ʿĀṣim, and al-Shaybānī, but absent from Ibn Buraydah, Qatādah, and Dāwūd), (3) an inverted interrogation sequence placing the Prophet and the political-religious description of Arab unity before the geographical inquiries ($P_5$), and (4) an abbreviated conclusion omitting the extended geographical sanctuary perimeter. This multi-feature alignment demonstrates that Mujālid's branch represents a distinct recensional sub-tradition within Kufa, rather than a single isolated scribal error. Neither pattern proves that these features originated with the named transmitters, but cladistic tracking across the Riwaq graph reveals that interrogation sequence acted as a flexible oral-transmission variable: even within a shared lower line (such as Sayyār–Qurrah), sequence diverges, indicating that order was not frozen at the Common Link stage [13, 15].

The protection of Ṭaybah/Medina and, in some versions, Mecca recurs, but endings differ in their elaboration of guarding angels and the Prophet's gesture indicating an eastern direction [1–4, 10]. The shorter texts must be read with their own abridgment notices in view. Absence from an abridged source is not equivalent to demonstrated omission from the transmitter's entire account.

## The related Jābir report

The Jābir witness in Abū Dāwūd 4328 presents sailors who exhaust their provisions, an encounter with al-Jassāsah, and a further dialogue identifying the Dajjāl with Ibn Ṣayyād [2]. This is a genuine companion-level difference within a connected narrative field. The Ibn Ṣayyād identification is reported as an attributed claim within the Jābir transmission, not as a historical conclusion endorsed by the present investigation. Counting this notice as another independent copy of the long Fāṭimah sermon would erase its different narrative frame and appendices. Keeping it as a comparator preserves both similarity and divergence.

## What Riwaq establishes, and what its scores cannot establish

*Methodological Triad:* In evaluating hadith transmission networks, three distinct analytical operations must be rigorously kept separate:
1. **Computational Collation:** Algorithmic alignment, graph generation, and metric calculation (such as pericope span tracking and token-Jaccard coherence), which verify formal textual overlaps and topological densities across indexed primary texts. Automated pericope alignment serves strictly within this category as a scalable computational collation tool that standardizes segment boundaries across voluminous texts, assisting rather than replacing philological analysis.
2. **Philological Interpretation:** Scholarly analysis of lexical registers, syntactic shifts, narrative motifs, and contextual nuances across surviving recensions.
3. **Historical Reconstruction:** Inductive inference regarding transmission horizons, historical provenance, and social context, which remains conditional on the degree to which surviving chains reflect actual transmission history.

The audited import contains eighteen encoded witnesses, twenty compiler-path variants, and seventy-one narrator profiles, with eighty-two graph edges reported in the retained pipeline review. Al-Ṭabarānī 967 initially remained outside the graph because its conjunction had not been resolved. In the initial unsegmented baseline run, Riwaq ranked al-Shaʿbī first with a structural score of **0.7025** (candidate outcome **`contested`**, fan-out **9**, bundle coverage **85.0%**, collector diversity **9**, bypass ratio **5.0%**), while Fāṭimah ranked second (score **0.4375**, outcome **`uncertain`**) and Ibn Buraydah third as a partial-common-link candidate (score **0.0900**, outcome **`likely_weak_in_context`**) [13].

Crucially, in that initial unsegmented run, **no imported variant contained machine-encoded `matn_segments`**, causing Riwaq's `computeNodeMatnCoherence()` to fall back to `default_constant_unmeasured` (a constant 0.50 weight). To overcome this foundational limitation, the Masar Takhrīj Engine's automated pericope alignment engine (`PericopeAligner`) was deployed across seven canonical narrative episodes ($P_1 \dots P_7$), injecting verified zero-based character spans directly into the analytical dataset. Re-executing the Riwaq pipeline with active matn segments activated empirical token-Jaccard coherence scoring (`measured_token_jaccard`). Furthermore, promoting two additional discovered witnesses from Masar's verified proposal buffer (al-Ṭabarānī 964 and 967) expanded the model to a 20-witness, 22-path corpus [15].

## Discussion

The sampled witnesses establish a recognizable island-and-Dajjāl narrative across multiple transmission configurations. Al-Shaʿbī is the central branching node of the dominant Fāṭimah family under both structural graph analysis and empirical matn-coherence testing; the independently named Abū Salamah and Yaḥyā ibn Yaʿmar approaches prevent an exclusive account of its transmission. The Jābir narrative deserves comparison at the motif level without collapsing its distinct companion attribution and Ibn Ṣayyād dialogue into Fāṭimah's sermon. Repetition across compilers frequently reflects shared lower chains rather than multiple independent early controls [1–10].

The integration of Masar's automated pericope alignment with Riwaq's `measured_token_jaccard` scoring provides a practical computational enhancement over initial unmeasured runs, standardizing episode segmentation across the corpus. With al-Shaʿbī achieving an empirical Common Link confidence of 0.6697 (19-attestation baseline) and 0.6743 (20-record expanded corpus), the computational data substantiate that a cohesive narrative tradition was transmitted through his circle. Furthermore, demonstrating that the kinsmen voyage spans four distinct pupil lines (Sayyār, Muḥammad ibn Ayyūb, al-Shaybānī, and Mujālid) demonstrates the utility of multi-branch pericope alignment to adjudicate transmission controversies on an empirical basis.

### The Historical Meaning of the Common Link: Transmission Horizons, Terminus Distinctions, and the Single Strand

In the methodological framework of Isnād-cum-Matn analysis (ICMA) pioneered by Joseph Schacht [16] and substantially refined by G.H.A. Juynboll [17], Harald Motzki [18], and Gregor Schoeler [19], the identification of a structural Common Link carries specific chronological and historiographical significance:

1. **Transmission Horizons and Terminus Distinctions:**
   In strict historical method, four chronological and material levels must be carefully distinguished:
   (a) *Physical Manuscript Horizon (Codicological Terminus Ante Quem):* Extant physical manuscript codices and material parchment/paper folios (which for the classical Sunni hadith compendia typically date from the fifth to seventh centuries AH / eleventh to thirteenth centuries CE, alongside earlier fragmentary papyri) establish the material *terminus ante quem*—the point before which surviving copies are physically preserved on material carriers.
   (b) *Literary Redaction Horizon (Compilation Terminus Ante Quem):* The third-century redaction and recorded death dates of the compilers (e.g., Muslim ibn al-Ḥajjāj, d. 261 AH / 875 CE; Abū Dāwūd al-Sijistānī, d. 275 AH / 889 CE) mark the scholastic horizon by which the individual traditions were compiled, organized, and disseminated in early scholarly literature.
   (c) *Documented Biographical Horizon:* The recorded lifespans of named authorities appearing in the isnād chains (such as ʿĀmir al-Shaʿbī, d. ca. 103–105 AH / 721–723 CE) mark when these figures lived according to classical biographical compendia (*kutub al-rijāl*).
   (d) *Reconstructed Transmission Horizon (Analytical Terminus a Quo for Surviving Branches):* Within the internal logic of Isnād-cum-Matn analysis, the structural convergence of nine distinct transmission lines upon al-Shaʿbī establishes an analytical transmission horizon. This indicates that—**if the attributed transmission relationships substantially reflect actual historical transmission**—the core narrative (encompassing the maritime voyage, the encounter with al-Jassāsah, the chained captive on the island, the fivefold geographical inquiries, and the sanctuary protection of Mecca and Medina) was in active public circulation in Kufa by the late first century AH (c. 75–100 AH / 695–718 CE). While this finding presents an empirical challenge to late Abbasid dating within the multi-branch transmission record, it remains methodologically conditional upon the premise that isnāds were not systematically back-projected downstream onto al-Shaʿbī.

2. **Common Link vs. Invention (The Historiographical Boundary):**
   Crucially, within contemporary ICMA methodology, identifying a Common Link does **not** equate to proving that this individual invented or fabricated the report. While early formulations by Schacht [16] and Juynboll [17] tended to equate the Common Link with the historical creator of a tradition, Harald Motzki's corrective demonstrated that a Common Link simply marks the earliest traditionist whose systematic teaching and dissemination resulted in surviving multi-branch transmission lines [18]. Whether al-Shaʿbī received the report orally from Fāṭimah bint Qays, an unnamed intermediary, or regional storytelling circles remains an unproven attribution claim. Graph topology alone cannot verify whether Tamīm al-Dārī experienced an actual maritime event or encountered a mythical beast; such historical questions lie beyond the evidentiary ceiling of isnād-cum-matn analysis.

3. **The Single Strand (SS) and Collateral Transmission:**
   In ICMA theory, the single strand connecting the Common Link to earlier authorities (Fāṭimah bint Qays $\to$ al-Shaʿbī) constitutes an *attribution claim* preserved by the Common Link. Because all downstream transmission lines in this cluster collapse into the bottleneck of al-Shaʿbī, downstream pupil branches do not provide independent eyewitness verification back to the Prophet. However, the survival of collateral, non-Shaʿbī lines through Abū Salamah in Medina (*Abū Dāwūd 4325*) and Yaḥyā ibn Yaʿmar in Khurasan (*Ibn Ḥibbān 6787*) supports an alternative multi-regional transmission history rather than an exclusive Kufan invention by al-Shaʿbī, though these collateral paths are fewer in number and remain dependent on the integrity of their own transmission records.

4. **Oral Fluidity vs. Monolithic Archetype:**
   The cross-branch distribution of traveler profiles ($P_2$) fundamentally clarifies the tradition's early evolution. Because four distinct pupil lines transmit the kinsmen reading (*banū ʿamm*), while Ibn Buraydah and Dāwūd preserve the Lakhmid personal voyage, the reconstructive model of ICMA suggests that both variants were circulating within al-Shaʿbī's broader teaching circle in Kufa by the late first century AH, refuting the suspicion that the kinsmen framing was an idiosyncratic corruption of Mujālid. Whether al-Shaʿbī personally varied his wording across different teaching sessions or whether his pupils introduced minor phrasing adaptations during oral transmission remains a conditional historical inference. Nonetheless, the evidence establishes that early transmission possessed natural oral flexibility—such as varying question orders, traveler descriptions, and lexical shifts—characteristic of early Islamic memory culture [19], rather than existing as a rigid, monolithic written archetype.

## Conclusion

**What the sources directly establish.** Within nineteen retained digital source attestations (expanded to twenty records yielding twenty-two compiler paths in the supplementary discovery), versions of the Jassāsah story share an identifiable maritime encounter while differing in sermon frame, traveler attribution, description of the encountered being, interrogation content and order, and explanatory additions. The dominant Fāṭimah–al-Shaʿbī transmission fans out through nine labeled pupil branches, alongside two other reported approaches to Fāṭimah and a distinct Jābir comparator [1–10, 15].

**What the isnād-cum-matn pattern suggests.** Al-Shaʿbī is the leading structural Common Link candidate across all tested models, with an empirical Isnād-cum-Matn confidence of **0.6697** in the 19-attestation baseline and **0.6743** in the expanded 20-record corpus under Riwaq's `measured_token_jaccard` profile. Narrative variation in traveler identity reveals multiple early formulations: the kinsmen voyage (*banū ʿamm*) is computationally confirmed across four distinct pupil branches radiating from al-Shaʿbī, refuting its exclusive attribution to Mujālid, while the Lakhmid companion voyage coexists on parallel lines (Ibn Buraydah, Dāwūd). Distinctive additions—such as the Persia interrogation in the Dāwūd–Ḥammād line—demonstrate branch-concentrated textual distributions across the surviving corpus, though determining their exact historical moment of authorial inception remains an analytical inference [15].

**What could materially change the conclusion.** Collation of physical manuscripts and unconsulted early corpora (e.g., al-Ḥumaydī, Isḥāq ibn Rāhwayh, Abū ʿAwānah, al-Bayhaqī, and al-Ṭaḥāwī) could verify whether the Persia question or specific descriptive terms appear in collateral early lines. Independent external dating evidence for named transmitters would refine chronological constraints.

**What remains uncertain or unverified.** No secure archetypal original text, absolute origination date, or independent historical verification of the island encounter can be deduced from graph topology or pericope alignment. The computational scores describe transmission coherence and branch structure, not historical veracity or report fabrication.

**Corpus coverage and limits.** Counts apply strictly to the audited digital corpus and its staged expansions in Masar and Riwaq. Full manuscript collation across diverse geographic recensions remains outstanding.

## Note of Caution and Methodological Disclaimer

While this study presents an empirical Isnād-cum-Matn analysis of the *al-Jassāsah* tradition where computational confidence mathematically accounts for exact textual divergence between unabridged reports (*Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* 2942a, *Sunan Ibn Mājah* 4074) and abridged compiler cross-references (*Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* 2942b–d, *Sunan Abī Dāwūd* 4327) using Masar and Riwaq, readers are advised to interpret these findings with methodological caution. 

Computational isnād graphs and algorithmic pericope alignments produce mathematical models of textual and transmission coherence; they do not generate theological verdicts, nor do they replace traditional Islamic biographical criticism (*ʿilm al-rijāl wa-al-jarḥ wa-al-taʿdīl*). The confidence metrics reflect structural density and lexical overlap within a defined digital sample rather than ontological certainty or historical truth. Consequently, these findings should be critically evaluated, verified, and contextualized by qualified Ḥadīth scholars and classical philologists before being relied upon for normative historical, theological, or legal conclusions.
